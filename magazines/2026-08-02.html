Exploring the Hidden Complexity of Elevator Systems

Source: original article

Everyone has shared the frustration of waiting for an elevator that never seems to arrive. "I pressed the button, why isn't it coming?" you ask. For something as commonplace as elevators, they are far more complex than meets the eye. Over the course of this article, we'll unravel the mysteries of elevators. The way you push their buttons, and how they push yours.

Actionable Insight

Despite their ubiquitous presence, elevators are far more complex than commonly perceived, involving intricate algorithms to manage passenger flow efficiently. The design of these systems significantly impacts user experience, often leading to frustration when algorithms fail to adapt to real-world scenarios like peak demand or user errors.

Community Voice

The community discusses various aspects of elevator algorithms, from simulating them in computer science projects and games (like 'Elevator Saga' and 'Sky Lobby') to real-world frustrations. Common issues include elevators stopping at every floor when already full, inefficient handling of large crowds (e.g., after conferences or in tourist-heavy buildings), and the perceived ineffectiveness of certain algorithms like 'Destination Dispatch.' Commenters also highlight the psychological impact of wait times and user behavior, such as pressing both 'up' and 'down' buttons, which can further complicate elevator scheduling.

Google's Practices Blamed for Decline in RSS Adoption

Source: original article

How Google helped destroy adoption of RSS feeds - Open RSS Although RSS feeds are alive and still heavily used today, their level of adoption has suffered because of how difficult a handful of popular technology companies have made it to use them. Google, especially, has relied on the open web RSS protocol to gain so much market share and influence, but continues to engage in behavior that exploits the open web at the expense of its users. As a result, Google has single-handedly contributed to the reason many users who once relied on RSS feeds have stopped using them. Below, we dive deeper into Google's track record of what appears to be an Embrace, Extend, and Extinguish model.

Actionable Insight

Google is accused of contributing significantly to the decline in RSS feed adoption, despite having benefited from the open web protocol. Its alleged 'Embrace, Extend, and Extinguish' strategy has made RSS difficult to use, leading many former users to abandon the technology. This behavior is seen as exploiting the open web at the expense of its users.

Community Voice

The community largely views Google's actions, particularly the shutdown of Google Reader, as a pivotal moment that signaled the end of a more open, less ad-driven internet. Many commenters believe Google's stated reasons for discontinuing RSS support were disingenuous, driven by a desire to eliminate non-monetizable content delivery. Despite these challenges, users emphasize that RSS is not dead, continuing to thrive within niche communities, and advocate for independent readers and broader site support for feeds, while also noting other platforms like Mozilla have also removed RSS features.

Internet Standards 🌐

RFC 10015 Deprecates Obsolete Key Exchange Methods in TLS 1.2 and DTLS 1.2

Source: original article

RFC 10015: Deprecating Obsolete Key Exchange Methods in TLS 1.2 and DTLS 1.2 Stream: Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) RFC: 10015 Updates: 4162 , 4279 , 4346 , 4785 , 5246 , 5288 , 5289 , 5469 , 5487 , 5932 , 6209 , 6347 , 6367 , 6655 , 7905 , 8422 , 9325 Category: Standards Track Published: July 2026 ISSN: 2070-1721 Author: Deprecating Obsolete Key Exchange Methods in TLS 1.2 and DTLS 1.2 For (D)TLS 1.2, this document deprecates the use of two key exchanges, namely Diffie-Hellman (DH) over a finite field and RSA. It also discourages the use of static Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDH) cipher suites.

Actionable Insight

RFC 10015, published by the IETF, formally deprecates Diffie-Hellman over a finite field and RSA key exchanges for TLS 1.2 and DTLS 1.2, while also discouraging static Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDH) cipher suites. This update aims to bolster the security of these widely used protocol versions by removing less robust cryptographic practices. The move reflects a continuous effort to enhance internet security standards, even for established protocols.

Community Voice

One commenter suggests that supporting TLS 1.3 is not difficult or costly, proposing that TLS 1.2 be marked as legacy and practical constraints be applied to TLS 1.3 implementations to prevent replay attacks.
