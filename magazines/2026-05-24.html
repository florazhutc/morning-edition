Reverse Engineering Spacelab's 1980 Pre-Microprocessor Computer Circuitry

Reverse engineering circuitry in a Spacelab computer from 1980 Reverse engineering circuitry in a Spacelab computer from 1980 Spacelab was a reusable laboratory that could be carried in the cargo bay of the Space Shuttle, providing lab space for astronauts and experiments. Spacelab was controlled by a French-built minicomputer, called the Mitra 125 MS . Unlike modern computers, this computer didn't contain a microprocessor chip.

Actionable Insight

This project offers a fascinating glimpse into the foundational era of computing and space technology, showcasing how complex systems were engineered without the benefit of integrated microprocessors. Reverse engineering such a system reveals architectures built from discrete logic components, highlighting the ingenuity and robust design practices required for critical aerospace applications of the early 1980s. Such endeavors are crucial for preserving historical technical knowledge and appreciating the remarkable evolution of computing hardware.

Community Voice

The community expresses significant admiration for the "insanely detailed" reverse engineering effort, highlighting the fascinating historical insights provided by delving into vintage Spacelab computer hardware. There's particular curiosity regarding the unique construction and design choices of the multi-layer PCBs from that era.

Transform Your Old Laptop into a Distraction-Free Writerdeck

A couple of weeks ago, I decided to convert my old laptop into a writerdeck , a dedicated writing device free from the distractions of the modern internet. Lots of folks build really elaborate offline devices for this, and I'd love to do that... Right now I have no shortage of projects and the point is to get writing, so I used what I had: a six-year-old laptop which still runs great, has plenty of power, but isn't getting much use anymore. Crucially, this laptop has an excellent keyboard, and a matte screen, which makes it awesome to type on for long stretches, and functional enough in the daylight (I like to sit outside with my dog and write). Now, you could certainly just use a regular desktop OS and keep it offline, although that's easier said than done.

Actionable Insight

The "writerdeck" concept offers a clever, low-cost solution to digital distraction by repurposing existing hardware into a dedicated, single-purpose tool for creative work. This reflects a growing trend towards digital minimalism, where the deliberate limitation of technology — often via older, offline devices — serves as a powerful feature to foster deeper focus and enhance productivity, rather than constantly seeking new or more powerful multi-purpose tools.

Community Voice

While acknowledging the technical impressiveness, the Hacker News community largely views the "writerdeck" as an over-engineered and potentially ironic solution to the problem of distraction. Many comments suggest the elaborate setup process itself could be a form of distraction or dopamine-chasing, questioning its long-term efficacy compared to simpler methods or underlying issues. This reflects a common pattern on HN where users elaborate technically intricate solutions for problems that might have more straightforward fixes.
