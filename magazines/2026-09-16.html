Capsule Bundles Single-File Web Apps with Integrated SQLite Data Storage

Source: Hacker News post

Hey HN, I always had the problem that building HTML pages is really simple now, but trying to save data required hosting it somewhere, and sharing it afterwards was not easy. Over the last few months, I've been building an app called Capsule (it’s also the file extension name) written in Rust with Tauri 2.0 that allows packing an HTML app and its data into a single SQLite file. The HTML file and any related assets are directly embedded in the database. Actionable Insight

Capsule addresses the challenge of data persistence and sharing for simple HTML apps by bundling the app and its data into a single SQLite file. Leveraging Rust and Tauri 2.0, it embeds HTML assets and user data, offering both key/value and document-style storage. This approach simplifies distribution and offline use, potentially making web apps more self-contained and portable.

Community Voice

The community noted that similar capabilities exist through browser APIs like the File System Access API and OPFS, which allow web pages to read/write local files and store data in SQLite. Some commenters expressed skepticism about the general applicability of bundling web apps into a specific executable, questioning its advantages over traditional hosted web apps or PWA approaches, especially for applications requiring frequent updates or complex state management. However, others recognized the concept as a modern iteration of older bundled application models (e.g., Clipper/dBase) and shared their own attempts or interest in building similar single-file, portable app solutions.

Mass Surveillance Criticized for Exceeding Post-9/11 Justifications Over 25 Years

Source: original article

25 Years of Mass Surveillance Is Enough - Schneier on Security This essay was written with Cindy Cohn, and originally appeared in Lawfare . One of the many legacies of the terrorist attacks of Sept. 11 is the government-wide shift from targeted surveillance—such as individual wiretaps or pen register/trap and trace orders—to mass surveillance techniques—such as tapping into the internet backbone or mass collection of telephone or internet metadata. The legal and technical architecture of modern mass surveillance, initially framed as a necessary defense against terrorist threats, has grown far beyond that justification and national security in general.

Actionable Insight

The article argues that mass surveillance, initially expanded after 9/11 for counter-terrorism, has grown far beyond its original national security justifications. It highlights a shift from targeted to pervasive surveillance techniques, establishing a legal and technical framework that critics now deem excessive after 25 years. The authors contend that the current scale of surveillance is no longer warranted.

Community Voice

Commenters express deep concern over the long-term societal impact of mass surveillance, viewing it as a path to totalitarian control and a 'downward spiral.' Many believe the desire for data collection is inherent in government, with the Patriot Act cited as a pivotal moment, and that 'temporary' surveillance powers inevitably become permanent. Proposed solutions include developing and widely distributing privacy-enhancing services and restricting federal access to local surveillance networks. Some also warn that surveillance might only cease if it becomes a national security vulnerability, while others point to new policies like NSPM-7 potentially increasing its scope.

Actionable Insight

The widespread breach of American driver's license data is being characterized as a national security disaster. This incident highlights significant vulnerabilities in identity verification systems and the potential for large-scale identity theft. Such a compromise could have far-reaching implications, including risks to individuals holding security clearances.

Community Voice

The community expresses deep frustration, advocating for increased personal liability for executives and investors of companies involved in breaches, and questioning the efficacy of Know Your Customer (KYC) checks given the rise of AI-generated fake documents. Commenters draw parallels to past national security breaches, like the 2015 OPM hack, to underscore a perceived lack of meaningful change. There's also a call to shift the burden of fraudulent activity, such as loans, from individuals to the financial institutions that issue them.
