Unix `*` Password Placeholder Exposes Magic String Vulnerabilities

Source: original article

back in old unix your password hash just sat in /etc/passwd , right next to your username. in 1980 bill joy put a * there for an account called daemon . nobody's supposed to ever log in as it. a * isn't a hash, so no password can match it. Address github.com/dspinellis/unix-history-repo/blob/BSD-3/etc/passwd

Actionable Insight

The historical Unix practice of using `*` in `/etc/passwd` to denote an unmatchable password illustrates the pitfalls of in-band signaling. This 'magic string' can be misinterpreted by authentication systems, potentially leading to vulnerabilities when cryptographic functions mishandle its special meaning. Modern software development should prioritize explicit error handling over overloaded string semantics to prevent such ambiguities.

Community Voice

Commenters highlight that the `*` character, intended to prevent password matches, creates a vulnerability due to in-band signaling, particularly in how `crypt` and `sshd` validate passwords. They criticize musl-libc for an incomplete workaround and advocate for a 'no magic strings' rule, preferring explicit failure values over special string interpretations. The discussion also touches on the broader argument that long-standing 'battle-tested' software isn't inherently secure due to accumulated cruft.

Source: original article

Bug Report: Privacy violation in User-Agent header · Issue #107757 · anthropics/claude-code · GitHub You signed in with another tab or window. Reload to refresh your session. You signed out in another tab or window. Reload to refresh your session.

Actionable Insight

Claude Code was found to embed user email addresses within HTTP User-Agent headers when making requests, raising significant privacy concerns. While some APIs might require such identification, this behavior occurred without explicit user consent in other contexts. Anthropic has acknowledged the issue and is implementing fixes to prevent the transmission of user emails.

Community Voice

Users reported an increase in spam emails, attributing it to Claude Code's behavior. The community questioned the extent of Claude's access to user details and external resources without explicit permission. While some noted that specific APIs, like the SEC API, mandate email in the User-Agent, Anthropic employees confirmed the privacy violation and announced a forthcoming update to remove email addresses from the model's context. A privacy plugin (`cc-plugin-privacy`) was also released in version 2.1.317+ as an initial mitigation.

Operating Systems & Hardware 💻

Hackintosh Explored: Running macOS on Non-Apple Hardware and the ARM Transition

Source: original article

A "Hackintosh" is the feat of running macOS on non-Apple hardware, and has a history dating back to the late 1980s. Almost a decade ago, I released a hackintosh guide on running macOS 10.14 through a VM with an x86 host. At the time KVM and PCI passthrough were relatively new technologies, and this guide allowed near-native performance with GPU acceleration. A few years passed by, and we saw Apple release the first ARM-based Mac in 2021, the M1 MacBook Air. The timing was impeccable, Intel was unable to create competitive processors (due to being stuck on the 14nm node), and Apple had powerful and efficient ARM chips already being shipped in their iPhones.

Actionable Insight

The Hackintosh movement, historically focused on running macOS on x86 non-Apple hardware, leveraged technologies like KVM and PCI passthrough for near-native performance. Apple's subsequent transition to ARM-based M-series chips marked a significant turning point, challenging the traditional Hackintosh approach while highlighting Apple's hardware innovation. This shift has redefined the technical hurdles and possibilities for running macOS outside of official Apple ecosystems.

Community Voice

Hacker News commenters largely express a preference for running Linux on Apple's highly-regarded hardware, citing dissatisfaction with macOS despite acknowledging the superior performance and design of Apple's M-series chips and laptops. Many recall positive past experiences with Hackintosh, while others look to projects like Asahi Linux for future compatibility. The discussion highlights a perception that Apple's primary value now resides in its hardware innovation rather than its operating system.
